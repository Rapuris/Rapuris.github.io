On a sunny Saturday morning, the Ellicott City Diner runs on momentum. Waitresses balance armfuls of plates, coffee pots keep moving, and the air smells like bacon grease and toast.

Frank Varon doesn’t need a menu. He orders what he always does when he wants a treat: the crab cake omelet. But when the check lands on the formica table, the number at the bottom stops him mid-sip.

The omelet costs $23.

“I remember when you could get a whole crab cake dinner for fifteen bucks,” Varon says, tapping the receipt. “Now, for a few eggs? You start asking if it’s worth it.”

He pays it anyway. In Maryland, craving blue crab isn’t really a preference; it’s closer to habit, something learned early and hard to shake. But $23 for a diner breakfast feels like a warning sign.

The numbers back that up. According to the <a href="https://news.maryland.gov/dnr/2025/05/22/maryland-department-of-natural-resources-reports-sustainable-population-in-2025-chesapeake-bay-blue-crab-winter-dredge-survey/">2025 Chesapeake Bay Blue Crab Winter Dredge Survey</a>, the population dropped to 238 million crabs—down 25 percent from the previous year and one of the lowest counts since tracking began in 1990.

“With more than five years of below-average crab numbers, it is clear that changing conditions in the bay are undermining the current management of this important species,” Allison Colden of the Chesapeake Bay Foundation said in a statement to <a href="https://www.seafoodsource.com/news/environment-sustainability/chesapeake-bay-blue-crab-population-dip-prompts-comprehensive-stock-assessment">SeafoodSource</a>.

For Baltimore, those aren’t abstract statistics. They show up on menus.

For Maria Costas, general manager of the Ellicott City Diner, they show up in her books.

“People come to a diner because they want good food that’s affordable,” Costas says, standing near the host stand as the late breakfast crowd blends into lunch. She gestures toward the kitchen. “But crab meat? It’s like buying gold now—except gold doesn’t go bad in three days and you can’t put it in an omelet.”

A few years ago, the crab cake omelet was a reliable moneymaker. Now it’s a loss leader she keeps on the menu for one reason: expectation.

“If I take crab off, we’re not a Maryland diner anymore,” she says. “But I’m getting close to $30. And then what?”

She already knows the answer. Tourists might pay it. Regulars won’t.

At <em>Michael’s Café</em> in Timonium, Chef Rosa Dantonio is dealing with the same math from a different angle. She sources crab from a waterman in Rock Hall whose family has worked the Bay for generations. Last summer, he called her with something she hadn’t heard before: doubt.

“He said the catch was so bad he didn’t know if it was worth going out,” she says. “This is someone who once went out in a nor’easter because he said that’s when the crabs were best.”

She kept her crab cake on the menu anyway, absorbing the higher costs. This year, she’s testing a backup: imported blue swimming crab from Indonesia. It works technically. It feels wrong.

To understand why this is happening, you don’t need a model—just a list. Warmer water. Dying seagrass. Runoff from farms. Invasive predators. Stack enough of those together and the system starts to break.

Gerald Winegrad, a former Maryland state senator, put it bluntly in an interview with the <a href="https://www.sierraclub.org/sierra/maryland-s-blue-crabs-red">Sierra Club</a>: Maryland isn’t just losing crabs; it’s losing part of its identity.

Back at the diner, the rush has thinned. Varon finishes the last bite of his omelet—rich, salty, dusted with Old Bay, exactly as he remembers.

“My grandfather used to say you weren’t really eating crabs unless your hands smelled for three days,” he says, pulling on his coat. “Now you spend so much you treat it like a museum piece. You don’t even want to get your hands dirty.”

He pauses at the door.

“I’ll keep coming back,” he says. “But next time, I might just get the bacon.”

A beat.

“Unless the pigs are having problems too.”

No one answers. The coffee pots keep moving.

Baltimore’s crab tradition was built on accessibility: newspaper on the table, Old Bay on your hands, something shared and unpretentious. That idea is slipping. As supply tightens and prices climb, blue crab is turning into something else—rarer, more expensive, less everyday.

The watermen are struggling. The restaurants are adjusting. And the people who grew up with crabs as a given are starting to hesitate at the menu.

Maria Costas isn’t ready to take the omelet off. Not yet.

“It stays,” she says, “until it can’t.”
