The first thing you should know is that “chai tea” is redundant. Chai is the Hindi word for tea. It comes from the Chinese chá, which traveled west through Persian and into Urdu and Hindi. So ordering a “chai tea latte” is, technically, asking for a “tea tea latte.” Nobody orders a “coffee coffee.” Nobody walks into a bakery and asks for “bread bread.” But here we are.

Chai, in its original form, is loose-leaf Assam black tea simmered in whole milk with crushed cardamom pods, fresh ginger, a cinnamon stick, maybe cloves, maybe black pepper, sugar added at the end and argued over at the table. That’s masala chai, “spiced tea,” and it is not what Starbucks is selling you for $6.25 a cup. For that price, you could buy a box of Assam tea and enough cardamom to last a month. It just wouldn’t come with a green straw and your name misspelled on the side.

*A chaiwala (tea vendor) pours masala chai in the traditional style. Photograph: Sampath Rapuri.*

What Starbucks is selling is a syrup. <a href="https://www.tazo.com/us/en/products/chai/classic-chai-latte-concentrate.html">Tazo Chai Concentrate</a>, to be exact: black tea, water, sweeteners, ginger extract, vanilla, cinnamon, and additives, mixed with steamed milk in a one-to-one ratio. It is fine. It is reliably fine. But it is also, depending on who you ask, either a gateway into South Asian flavors or a flattened version of them—sweet and approachable in ways that risk making the original invisible.

![Tazo’s Classic Chai Concentrate contains cane sugar and additives.](/assets/images/tazo_toxic.png)
*Tazo’s Classic Chai Concentrate contains cane sugar, high fructose corn syrup, and additives. Photograph: Sampath Rapuri.*

“I grew up drinking chai every morning before school,” Arindam Bagga, a junior at Johns Hopkins studying public health, told me. We were sitting in the student center with our respective cups: he had coffee, black; I had, somewhat self-consciously, a chai latte from downstairs. “When I see people at Starbucks ordering ‘chai tea’ and pronouncing it very carefully, I don’t know what to feel. It’s not offensive, exactly. It’s more like the thing they’re drinking and the thing I grew up with have nothing to do with each other. They just share a name.”

This disconnect—the original and the adaptation existing side by side without quite touching—is not unique to chai. American food culture has a long history of reshaping immigrant cuisines. Chinese food in the 19th century, Italian food in the 20th, and countless others have been streamlined, sweetened, or simplified as they moved into the mainstream.

The chai latte’s origin story runs through Seattle, not Mumbai. In 1994, Heather Howitt founded Oregon Chai, one of the first companies to sell pre-made chai concentrate in the United States. The product was built for coffee shops: quick, consistent, shelf-stable. Starbucks adopted it, and the drink spread. By the early 2000s, the “chai tea latte” was as American as the drive-through it was handed through. The spice profile softened, the sweetness increased, and ginger—that sharp, almost medicinal bite—faded into the background.

As <a href="https://www.theguardian.com/lifeandstyle/2016/feb/19/chai-latte-the-all-conquering-beverage-causing-a-storm-in-a-coffee-cup">reported in The Guardian</a>, former Starbucks brand manager Paul Williams said there were internal debates about whether to call it a “latte” at all. He argued that Starbucks’ real strength is not invention but translation: taking niche products and making them mainstream.

Food scholar Krishnendu Ray puts it more bluntly in the same piece. The Western chai latte, he argues, reflects what happens when a cuisine is filtered through a desire for the exotic, its most recognizable traits exaggerated until the result becomes less a drink and more “a clutter of spices.”

“What they sell is basically a dessert,” said Aakash Mehta, a visiting PhD student in mechanical engineering who grew up in Pune. “My mom wouldn’t recognize it as chai. But that’s okay. You can like both things. They’re just different.” It felt like he was giving me permission, and I took it—I was on my second chai latte of the day.

This is the more generous reading, and probably the more useful one. Cultural borrowing isn’t inherently theft. Sometimes it’s closer to translation, and translation always involves loss. The problem is scale. When the translated version becomes dominant, the original risks disappearing. People learn the word “chai” from a Starbucks menu and assume that’s the whole story.

It isn’t. The whole story involves a pot on a gas stove, cardamom opening in hot milk, ginger hitting the back of your throat, and someone’s mother insisting you haven’t had enough. The cardboard cup is just a postcard from that place. Worth savoring, maybe—but not the destination.
