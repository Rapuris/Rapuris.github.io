The parsnips at the Cottingham Farm stand look like they have been through something. Knobbed, forked, caked in dried mud, they sit in a wooden crate next to a pile of storage carrots so gnarled they could pass for ginger root. A hand-lettered sign reads: 
OVERWINTERED IN THE GROUND — SWEETER THAN YOU THINK.

![The Waverly market in Baltimore during the transition season.](/assets/images/waverly_2.png)
*The Waverly market in Baltimore during the transition season.*

It is the last Saturday of March at the 32nd Street Farmers Market in Waverly, and the stalls tell a split story. Half the vendors are selling the tail end of winter — root vegetables pulled from cold storage, frozen pork from last fall's slaughter, eggs from hens just now returning to full production after the short days of January. The other half are selling the promise of spring: trays of tomato seedlings, herb starts in four-inch pots, seed garlic for planting. What almost nobody is selling is fresh spring produce. It does not exist yet.

Farmers call this window the hungry gap. It is the stretch between mid-March and late April when the storage crops are running low and the spring plantings have not yet come in. For shoppers accustomed to grocery stores, where the asparagus aisle looks the same in January as it does in June, the gap is invisible. At a farmers market, it is the whole show.

"People come out on the first warm Saturday expecting salad greens and strawberries," says Janet Mullins, who grows mixed vegetables on twelve acres in Harford County. She gestures at her table: potatoes, celeriac, a few bunches of overwintered kale with leaves thick as leather. "This is what March actually looks like."

Mullins has learned to make the gap work. She plants spinach under row cover in October so it overwinters and produces a few early cuttings by April. She keeps sweet potatoes in a curing barn through the cold months. And she has started growing microgreens in a basement setup — trays of radish and sunflower shoots that offer something bright and alive when everything else on the table is brown and dense.

The microgreens sell out first every week. "People want green," Mullins says. "They're desperate for it."

Three stalls away, a shopper named Andre Wallace holds a bunch of overwintered parsnips in one hand and a bag of frozen ground beef in the other. He has been coming to Waverly since 2021 and says the late-March market took some getting used to. "The first year, I showed up and was like, where's the lettuce?" he says. Now he sees the sparse weeks differently. "It makes you appreciate the cycle. When the snap peas finally show up in May, it actually means something."

That shift in expectation is part of what market advocates say makes farmers markets valuable beyond the food itself. Buying seasonally means confronting what the land is actually doing at any given moment — including the moments when it is doing very little. The hungry gap is not a failure of the system. It is the system.

By 10 a.m., Mullins is down to a single crate of potatoes and a few bags of microgreens. A woman stops and picks up the celeriac, turning the ugly root in her hands like she is appraising a baseball.

"What do you do with this?" she asks.

"Soup," Mullins says. "Peel it, cube it, simmer it with a potato and some stock. You won't believe it."

The woman looks skeptical. She buys it anyway.
